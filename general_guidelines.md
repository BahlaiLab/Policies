# General Lab Policies and Guidelines
Welcome to the [Bahlai Lab of Applied Computational Ecology](https://bahlailab.org/)! I'm very excited that you're here. In this lab, we study a variety of habitats, taxa and processes, with the core elements of using data, math and computational techniques to gain better understanding of ecological systems. We're committed to applying what we learn to making the world a better place- by supporting sustainability and productivity in working ecosystems, by making the work we do accessible to the broader community, and by drawing in diverse viewpoints to enhance mutual understanding of science, scientists, and the needs of the world.  

## Open philosophy
First and foremost, the Bahlai Lab is an [open science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_science) lab. There are several major facets to this, but the core is: *the way this lab operates is grounded in my personal belief that participation in science is a public act, and done in the public good.* Science allows us to be part of something bigger, and to foster a *healthy, collaborative and truly inclusive and equitable scientific enterprise, it's essential that we give back at least as much as we take from the community*. I could get down into the weeds about specific tools and techniques that we use in our open science infrastructure here, but those tools are used to support these values, [they themselves are not the values](https://www.americanscientist.org/article/open-science-isnt-always-open-to-all-scientists). What we commit to do, as open scientists, is:

* Seek out expertise from conventional and unconventional contributors in our work. Invite comment and participation. Maintain a dialogue. Welcome feedback. Break down the barriers that prevent any and all interested parties from engaging in your corner of science.

* Make supporting research data freely available whenever possible, to support future use in meta-analyses, reviews, and revisitations of our work. 

* Respect privacy and confidentiality in cases where data or research products contain sensitive information, or information that otherwise does not belong to you. Do no harm. 

* Produce and share reproducible, re-usable data manipulation and analysis code, so people can understand our assumptions and workflows, and so future scientists can learn from our efforts without duplicating them.

* Publish final manuscripts AND intermediate research products in the most universally accessible formats available to us. 

* Acknowledge contributions to our work. Cite the ideas of others. Don't pretend we work in a vacuum. Use your platform to lift others and give them a platform, too.

* Act as ambassadors of open science, and science in general, to the broader scientific community and the world. Help people see what we do, but *respect the constraints others must work under.*

## Mentoring philosophy  
I believe a mentor's role is to serve as a guide and source of support as you work toward your goals. I strive to maintain an open dialogue about where you want to go, what you hope to achieve, and how I can help you get there. I will provide direction, resources, opportunities, and connections whenever I can, and help you find the right people when I can't. I believe the most important product of science is human capital: people who can think critically, work hard, and make meaningful contributions to their communities. I am invested in helping you develop as a colleague, scientist, and citizen of the world. In return, I ask that you bring curiosity, effort, openness to growth, and a commitment to science, truth, and kindness. My goal is not simply that you earn a degree, but that you leave this lab more capable, independent, knowledgeable, and confident than when you arrived.

I expect all lab members to maintain a commitment to professionalism. By professionalism, I do not mean conforming to the aesthetics or ways of being used to police people who differ from norms or expectations. I mean taking your development as a scientist and community member seriously: showing up, contributing, treating others with respect, engaging honestly and ethically with your work, and investing in your own growth. Expertise is built intentionally over time through reading, participation, practice, and reflection. Your education, research, and development should be priorities, even if they are not your only priorities.

I prefer to be a cheerleader rather than a boss, though sometimes being a boss is part of the job. My role is to support, challenge, advocate for, and occasionally redirect you. Your role is to take ownership of your development and your research. Graduate school is different from coursework. There is no answer key and rarely a single correct next step. Learning to make decisions despite uncertainty is part of becoming a scientist. I cannot make you the world expert on your subject, but you can. Read deeply. Ask questions. Seek feedback. Think creatively. Develop opinions. Change them when the evidence warrants it.

Science is the process of figuring out how the world works, building on what others have discovered before us. You will be wrong, often. So will I. Learning to live productively with uncertainty and occasional failure is part of the job. I generally give positive feedback profusely and negative feedback as redirection. If I suddenly suggest a dramatically different approach, it is usually because I think you are stuck and need a path forward. I may also be spectacularly wrong. Tell me why and cite your sources.

Finally, I believe science is better when it is diverse, inclusive, and accessible. People encounter different barriers, and equal treatment is not always equitable treatment. If challenges are affecting your ability to learn, work, or contribute fully, please let me know at whatever level of detail you are comfortable sharing. Together, we can identify resources and strategies that help you succeed.

## Meetings and feedback

A quick lab norm I want to flag: I'm very prone to going down interesting rabbit holes in conversation, which means meetings aren't always the most efficient way for me to give feedback. (I will literally follow any aside! See? I'm doing it now. I'll also infodump. Don't get me going. Beware.) To make sure your needs are actually met, please send me any writing, figures, analyses, code, or other materials ahead of time, along with a short list of specific questions or decisions you want feedback on. That way, when we do meet, we can focus on what you actually need, and in many cases I can give better feedback asynchronously. Part of this is that I genuinely need time to think. My strongest scientific feedback usually comes after I've had a chance to sit with a problem, and I'm just better at writing corrections.

Relatedly, context matters. I'm not very useful when shown an isolated paragraph, figure, or line of code and asked whether it's "right." First, I don't believe in the concept of  "right" as something immutable- it's 'less wrong' (All models are wrong, some are useful. -George Box), it's based on our current knowledge, it's good enough.  Most useful feedback depends on understanding what you're trying to accomplish and how that particular piece fits into the larger project (My feedback on writing tends to be INTENSE- this is not a condemnation- it's to help us work together to create a very good product.)

Finally, I encourage everyone to make reasonable use of available resources before asking for help. Documentation, published methods, package vignettes, Google, and your peers are all valuable resources. Part of becoming an independent researcher is learning how to find answers, evaluate them, and determine where you're genuinely stuck. I grew up both academically and socially in a fairly "figure it out and report back" environment, and that experience has probably shaped my expectations more than is entirely fair. As a result, I'm most helpful when you've already kicked the tires a bit and can tell me not just what's confusing, but what's confusing after you've tried to answer the question yourself.

## How to move through academia without making yourself (and everyone around you) miserable

Welcome to academic culture! We've got rules, traditions, unspoken norms, and a bunch of people whose brains, on average, work a bit differently than 'normal people' in 'normal society.' This means our enterprise is an endless supply of problems, some universal and some very specific to our fields, places or relationships. Some are important. Some are hypothetical. Some are mildly annoying. Some are actively harmful.  Some are artifacts of institutions being institutions.  One of the most important professional skills you can develop is learning which ones deserve your attention. _Said without cynicism: you can't fix it all. You can't control it all. Conserve energy for your priorities. Not everything can be a priority._

I say this as someone who can identify things that are inefficient, illogical, or poorly designed like it's my job. It _is_ kind of my job. This is a superpower for reviewing research. It's also a symptom of an anxiety disorder. Once you've spent enough time anticipating a possible future problem, it's surprisingly easy to start treating it as an actual one. The more energy you invest, the more urgent it can start to feel. Something else I've learned, often the hard way, is that urgency is contagious. When one person starts treating something as urgent, other people interrupt their work, reshuffle priorities, and spend attention responding to it. Over time, that kind of urgency doesn't stay confined to the original problem, it shapes the culture around it. A space where everything is treated as a crisis becomes a group that is constantly anxious, reactive, and exhausted. People start spending more energy managing alarm than solving problems. They actually get less done, overall.

We are a group of smart people with unusually active brains. We do better when we are thoughtful about which concerns deserve escalation and which deserve perspective. In my experience, almost nothing we do is truly urgent. Important, sure. Time-sensitive, sometimes. But unless someone is hurt, at risk of being hurt, facing a serious ethical issue, or otherwise in genuine danger, we're probably dealing with a planning problem rather than a crisis. We are scientists, not the other kind of doctors.

Flexibility and prioritization matter. Sooner or later everyone needs patience, understanding, quick feedback, flexibility, or a favor from colleagues. _**Do not spend that goodwill on things that don't genuinely need it.**_ It's also worth remembering that not every problem is your problem. Some issues belong to other people. Some require expertise you don't yet have. Some are real problems, just not the **most important** ones right now. Recognizing a problem does not automatically create an obligation to solve it. Not every problem needs a solution. Not every solution needs a project. Not every project needs to start today. And not every irritation deserves to become a crusade. _Christie, did you hear that? Put the pitchfork down._

## Authorship and credit

I view authorship as recognition of intellectual labor and contribution and we generally use it as a productivity metric in the broader academic sphere, so it's important for your career trajectory (because capitalism). 

As a general rule, the person who leads a project is the first author. Leadership may include developing the idea, carrying out the work, coordinating collaborators, performing analyses, writing the manuscript, or some combination of these activities. Work conducted under my supervision and using substantial lab resources will typically include me as the senior (last) author. "Lab resources" includes funding (grant and internal), personnel, equipment, institutional support, datasets, research infrastructure, and intellectual contributions to project development and execution. Not every project will look the same, but if a project is being carried out as part of the lab's research program, it is generally appropriate for me to serve as senior author. I view first authorship as reflecting leadership of the work and last authorship as reflecting responsibility for the environment that made the work possible. Maintaining funding, infrastructure, mentorship, collaborations, and institutional support is part of my job, as is being accountable for the work produced by the lab. Senior authorship is therefore not only a form of recognition, but also a reflection of responsibility. Corresponding authorship (usually the person who finalizes and submits the manuscript) is something in between- it signals leadership for completing and refining the product.

After that, I tend to be fairly liberal in assigning authorship. If you made a meaningful intellectual, analytical, methodological, writing, or data contribution to a project,  I believe we should generally err on the side of inclusion and recognition rather than exclusion- collaborative works between lab members, undergrad assistants, and external collaborators are encouraged. Authorship is not pie, giving a slice to others that contributed to the work does not deplete anyone else's slices. For authors in the middle of the list, I aim to balance contribution with equity. Contributions matter, but I also recognize that opportunities, confidence, seniority, and structural barriers are not distributed equally. Following principles similar to those outlined by [Liboiron and colleagues](https://civiclaboratory.nl/2016/05/23/equity-in-author-order/), authorship discussions should be transparent, collaborative, and attentive to fairness as well as productivity.

I expect authorship to be discussed early, revisited as projects evolve, and negotiated in good faith. The goal is not merely to assign credit, but to recognize the work people have done while building a collaborative and supportive research environment. When in doubt, we will talk about it. Authorship disputes are much easier to prevent than to resolve.

Authorship expectations do not expire when someone graduates, changes institutions, or otherwise leaves the lab. Contributions remain contributions, and authorship should continue to reflect the work people have done regardless of where they are currently employed. However, research projects also carry obligations. If a project remains inactive for an extended period after a student or researcher leaves, I may choose to move the project forward so that the work, data, and resources invested in it can continue to contribute to science (see open science policy above). As a general guideline, projects that have seen no substantial progress for approximately one year may be considered inactive.

In these situations, original contributors will continue to receive appropriate authorship credit for their contributions. However, if additional personnel undertake substantial work to complete, analyze, write, or publish the project, authorship order may be revisited to reflect those new contributions. In particular, taking responsibility for advancing an orphaned project to publication may warrant changes in author order, including first authorship where appropriate. My goal is not to take projects away from people, but to ensure that valuable research does not remain unpublished indefinitely- that hurts all of us! Whenever possible, these discussions will occur openly and collaboratively before any changes are made.

## Grad student-specific expectations
*This may seem obvious, but to be clear: **to work in my lab, I expect all graduate students to have strong interests in insects** (and/or other taxa, I'm cool with plants and stuff), **math and modelling and programming and informatics and quantitative ecology, and drive for doing original work that advances science.** I also want to you think about why you want a graduate degree- how does this program and this research advance your personal goals?* I am here to support you but I also have very high expectations for you- I expect all of my students to be driven: to do, to learn, and to make, in pursuit of their goals. Graduate school is not about doing everything. It is about identifying what matters to you and pursuing it with intention. (Wanna know the cheat code for winning grad school? Focus on doing a few things very, very, very well. Too many things done superficially are fluff). I expect you to be committed to immersing yourself in your program: I want you to have a full life with friends and family and hobbies but a graduate program, by its nature, will take a lot of you, and a lot of time. This IS A BIG COMMITMENT and you need to be both available and open for it. We can do amazing things with your work but the ultimate drive needs to come from you. *I will support you through a graduate program but I will not drag you through one. That's unpleasant for everybody.*

The expectations of graduate students enrolled in our MS and PhD programs in the [Kent State University Department of Biological Sciences](https://www.kent.edu/biology) are outlined in our [graduate student handbook](https://du1ux2871uqvu.cloudfront.net/sites/default/files/file/BSCI%20Grad%20Handbook%202017.pdf). The back of this guide contains links to specific forms, including a checklist of degree completion requirements for both the MS and PhD programs. We will use this as a [template for your individual expectations within the lab](https://github.com/BahlaiLab/Policies/blob/master/Graduate_programs.md). I generally expect incoming PhD students to come in with an MS or equivalent research or work experience. Incoming MS students may decide to matriculate into a PhD program at a later date, if that is their goal, in consultation with me, their committee, and given good research progress early in their program.
