# General Lab Policies and Guidelines
Welcome to the [Bahlai Lab of Applied Computational Ecology](https://bahlailab.org/)! I'm very excited that you're here. In this lab, we study a variety of habitats, taxa and processes, with the core elements of using data, math and computational techniques to gain better understanding of ecological systems. We're committed to applying what we learn to making the world a better place- by supporting sustainability and productivity in working ecosystems, by making the work we do accessible to the broader community, and by drawing in diverse viewpoints to enhance mutual understanding of science, scientists, and the needs of the world.  

## Open philosophy
First and foremost, the Bahlai Lab is an [open science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_science) lab. There are several major facets to this, but the core is: *the way this lab operates is grounded in my personal belief that participation in science is a public act, and done in the public good.* Science allows us to be part of something bigger, and to foster a *healthy, collaborative and truly inclusive and equitable scientific enterprise, it's essential that we give back at least as much as we take from the community*. I could get down into the weeds about specific tools and techniques that we use in our open science infrastructure here, but those tools are used to support these values, [they themselves are not the values](https://www.americanscientist.org/article/open-science-isnt-always-open-to-all-scientists). What we commit to do, as open scientists, is:

* Seek out expertise from conventional and unconventional contributors in our work. Invite comment and participation. Maintain a dialogue. Welcome feedback. Break down the barriers that prevent any and all interested parties from engaging in your corner of science.

* Make supporting research data freely available whenever possible, to support future use in meta-analyses, reviews, and revisitations of our work. 

* Respect privacy and confidentiality in cases where data or research products contain sensitive information, or information that otherwise does not belong to you. Do no harm. 

* Produce and share reproducible, re-usable data manipulation and analysis code, so people can understand our assumptions and workflows, and so future scientists can learn from our efforts without duplicating them.

* Publish final manuscripts AND intermediate research products in the most universally accessible formats available to us. 

* Acknowledge contributions to our work. Cite the ideas of others. Don't pretend we work in a vacuum. Use your platform to lift others and give them a platform, too.

* Act as ambassadors of open science, and science in general, to the broader scientific community and the world. Help people see what we do, but *respect the constraints others must work under.*

## Mentoring philosophy  
I believe that the role of a mentor is to serve as a guide and a source of support as you work toward your goals. I strive to maintain an open dialogue about where you want to go, what you hope to achieve, and how I can help you get there. I will provide direction, resources, opportunities, and connections whenever I can, and connect you with people who can help when I'm not the right person. I believe the most important product of science is human capital: people who can go forward into the world, think critically, work hard, and make meaningful contributions to their communities. I am deeply invested in helping you develop as a colleague, a scientist, and a citizen of the world. I ask that you bring a willingness to work hard and think hard, openness to growth and change, and a commitment to science, truth, and kindness. I will endeavor to bring those same things to our relationship. Because I see developing scientists as one of the most important outcomes of science, I take mentoring seriously. That also means I have high expectations. My goal is not simply that you earn a degree. My goal is that you leave this lab more capable, more independent, more knowledgeable, and more confident than when you arrived. 

I expect all lab members to maintain a commitment to professionalism. I know that "professionalism" is a loaded term and is often used to enforce conformity. That's not what I mean here. To me, professionalism means taking your development as a scientist and community member seriously. It means showing up, contributing, treating colleagues with respect, engaging with your work honestly and ethically, and investing in your own growth. It means reading deeply, participating in the broader scientific community, and recognizing that expertise is something you build intentionally over time. It means making your education, research, and development a priority. Not necessarily your only priority, but one that deserves real time and attention.

I prefer to be a cheerleader rather than a boss. My role is to support, challenge, advocate for, and occasionally redirect you. Your role is to take ownership of your development and your research. Graduate school is different from coursework. There is no answer key, and there is rarely a single correct next step. Much of the work involves learning how to make decisions and move forward despite uncertainty. I can't make you the world expert on what you do, but you can, and that is your job now. Read deeply. Ask questions. Seek feedback. Think creatively. Develop opinions. Change them when the evidence warrants it.

We don't have right answers. We chip away at figuring out how things work, in conversation with what other people before us have discovered. Learning to thrive at the edge of the unknown, where the answer is genuinely unclear, is what science is to me and what I hope it becomes for you. You will be wrong, often. So will I. (If being wrong occasionally is intolerable to you, science is a deeply unfortunate career choice because oy). 
I generally give positive feedback profusely and negative feedback in the form of redirection. This is partly temperament and partly philosophy. I am much more interested in helping you move forward than in dwelling on what isn't working or spiraling around the same problem. If I suddenly start suggesting dramatically different approaches, it is usually because I think you are stuck and need a way forward. I may be spectacularly wrong- tell me why and cite your sources!

Finally, I believe science is better when it is diverse, inclusive, and accessible. That means recognizing that people encounter different barriers and that equal treatment is not always equitable treatment. If you are facing challenges that affect your ability to learn, work, or contribute fully, please let me know in whatever level of detail you are comfortable sharing. Together, we can work with available resources to identify accommodations and strategies that help you succeed.

## Meetings and feedback

A quick lab norm I want to flag: I'm very prone to going down interesting rabbit holes in conversation, which means meetings aren't always the most efficient way for me to give feedback. (I will literally follow any aside! See? I'm doing it now. I'll also infodump. Don't get me going. Beware.) To make sure your needs are actually met, please send me any writing, figures, analyses, code, or other materials ahead of time, along with a short list of specific questions or decisions you want feedback on. That way, when we do meet, we can focus on what you actually need, and in many cases I can give better feedback asynchronously. Part of this is that I genuinely need time to think. My strongest scientific feedback usually comes after I've had a chance to sit with a problem, and I'm just better at writing corrections.

Relatedly, context matters. I'm not very useful when shown an isolated paragraph, figure, or line of code and asked whether it's "right." Most useful feedback depends on understanding what you're trying to accomplish and how that particular piece fits into the larger project (My feedback on writing tends to be INTENSE- this is not a condemnation- it's to help us work together to create a very good product.)

Finally, I encourage everyone to make reasonable use of available resources before asking for help. Documentation, published methods, package vignettes, Google, and your peers are all valuable resources. Part of becoming an independent researcher is learning how to find answers, evaluate them, and determine where you're genuinely stuck. I grew up both academically and socially in a fairly "figure it out and report back" environment, and that experience has probably shaped my expectations more than is entirely fair. As a result, I'm most helpful when you've already kicked the tires a bit and can tell me not just what's confusing, but what's confusing after you've tried to answer the question yourself.

## How to move through academia without making yourself (and everyone around you) miserable

Welcome to academic culture! We've got rules, traditions, unspoken norms, and a bunch of people whose brains, on average, work a bit differently than 'normal people' in 'normal society.' This means our enterprise is an endless supply of problems, some universal and some very specific to our fields, places or relationships. Some are important. Some are hypothetical. Some are mildly annoying. Some are actively harmful.  Some are artifacts of institutions being institutions.  One of the most important professional skills you can develop is learning which ones deserve your attention. _Said without cynicism: you can't fix it all. You can't control it all. Conserve energy for your priorities. Not everything can be a priority._

I say this as someone who can identify things that are inefficient, illogical, or poorly designed like it's my job. It's kind of my job. This is a superpower for reviewing research. It's also a symptom of an anxiety disorder. Once you've spent enough time anticipating a possible future issue, it's surprisingly easy to start treating it as an actual one, and the more energy you put into it, the more urgent it seems. Something else I've learned, often the hard way, is that urgency is contagious. When one person starts treating something as urgent, that urgency tends to spread outward. Other people interrupt their work, reshuffle priorities, and spend attention responding to it. It makes anxiety contagious. It consumes other people's finite energy, attention, resources, and goodwill.

We are a group of smart people with unusually active brains. We do better when we are thoughtful about which concerns deserve escalation and which deserve perspective. In my experience, almost nothing we do is truly urgent. Important, sure; time-sensitive, sometimes. But unless someone is hurt, at risk of being hurt, facing a serious ethical issue, or otherwise in genuine danger, we're probably not dealing with an emergency. We are scientists, not the other kind of doctors.

Flexibility and prioritization matter. Sooner or later everyone needs patience, understanding, quick feedback, flexibility, or a favor from colleagues. People are much more willing to provide those things when they know you reserve urgency for situations that genuinely warrant it. Not every problem needs a solution. Not every solution needs a project. Not every project needs to start today. And not every irritation deserves to become a crusade. _Christie, did you hear that? Put the pitchfork down._

## Grad student-specific expectations
*This may seem obvious, but to be clear: **to work in my lab, I expect all graduate students to have strong interests in insects** (and/or other taxa, I'm cool with plants and stuff), **math and modelling and programming and informatics and quantitative ecology, and drive for doing original work that advances science.** I also want to you think about why you want a graduate degree- how does this program and this research advance your personal goals?* I am here to support you but I also have very high expectations for you- I expect all of my students to be driven: to do, to learn, and to make, in pursuit of their goals. Graduate school is not about doing everything. It is about identifying what matters to you and pursuing it with intention. I expect you to be committed to immersing yourself in your program: I want you to have a full life with friends and family and hobbies but a graduate program, by its nature, will take a lot of you, and a lot of time. This IS A BIG COMMITMENT and you need to be both available and open for it. We can do amazing things with your work but the ultimate drive needs to come from you. *I will support you through a graduate program but I will not drag you through one. That's unpleasant for everybody.*

The expectations of graduate students enrolled in our MS and PhD programs in the [Kent State University Department of Biological Sciences](https://www.kent.edu/biology) are outlined in our [graduate student handbook](https://du1ux2871uqvu.cloudfront.net/sites/default/files/file/BSCI%20Grad%20Handbook%202017.pdf). The back of this guide contains links to specific forms, including a checklist of degree completion requirements for both the MS and PhD programs. We will use this as a [template for your individual expectations within the lab](https://github.com/BahlaiLab/Policies/blob/master/Graduate_programs.md). I generally expect incoming PhD students to come in with an MS or equivalent research or work experience. Incoming MS students may decide to matriculate into a PhD program at a later date, if that is their goal, in consultation with me, their committee, and given good research progress early in their program.
